### 1.2 Data model

- **Fare** = price for one-way travel in a **market** (city pair), "usually good for travel in either direction", identified by a fare basis code, published with rules. Example: `AA BOS-SFO H14ESNR $436.28`.
- **Axioms**: each flight is covered by exactly one fare; one fare may cover one or more (usually consecutive) flights; one or more fares pay for the journey.
- **Fare component (FC)** = a fare plus the flights it covers. The worked example prices SFO-BOS / BOS-DFW-SFO either as two Y fares ($2,000) or as Y + QE14 + K21AP ($1,800).
- **Priceable unit (PU)** = 1 to 4 fare components in a fixed geometry: one way, round trip, open jaw (two forms), circle trip of 3, circle trip of 4. An open jaw is a circle trip with one FC missing, and the open gap must usually be shorter than each of the flown FCs. A ticket is one or more PUs.
- The PU is the **domain of rules** such as minimum stay and advance purchase. The paper's example of a Saturday-night rule requires a Saturday night between the first departure of the PU's first fare component and the first departure of its last.
- **"Round trip fares"** are fares whose rules prohibit use in a one-way PU. They carry the Saturday-night restriction, so using one requires at least two fares and a Saturday night.
- The same flights partition into FCs and PUs in many ways, and the partition changes rule outcomes. In the SFO-DEN-BOS-DFW-SFO example, the 14-day advance purchase on the BOS-DFW QE14 fare measures to departure from SFO under one circle-trip PU, but to the later departure from DEN under two open-jaw PUs. The Saturday-night test flips the other way. The paper's conclusion: "Priceable units introduce long-distance flight & fare dependencies".
- **Routing** is "a directed graph of permitted routes within the fare component". H14ESNR permits nonstop BOS-SFO or stops in NYC, CHI, DFW, LAX, but not both DFW and CHI, at the same $436.28. The paper does not discuss the mileage system.
- **IATA checks** for international pricing can raise the total price of a ticket's fares to the highest price of any other set of fares that is similar enough, even when that other set is not itself a valid ticket. The paper declines to detail them.

### 1.3 Rules the paper describes

The paper does not use ATPCO category numbers. It organises restrictions by the scope they test:

| Scope | What rules can restrict (paper's list) |
|---|---|
| Passengers | age, nationality, occupation, employer, frequent flyer status |
| Fare component | dates, times, locations, airlines, flights, duration of stops |
| Priceable unit | PU type; other fares in the PU (airline and basis codes); dates, times, locations, airlines, flights, stop durations |
| Journey | fares and flights in other PUs (airline and basis codes); other PU geometries |
| Other | purchase location and time; which travel agents may sell |

Specific mechanisms it names:

- **Advance purchase**, measured from reservation to the first flight of the PU. A fare paying for a return 21 days out can fail a 14-day rule because the outbound was 7 days out.
- **Saturday-night / minimum stay**, and **minimum and maximum stay** generally (used in the EXPSPACE proof with min = max).
- **Day of week**: H14ESNR requires the first flight of the FC to depart Tuesday or Wednesday.
- **Surcharges**: H14ESNR adds $22.50 BOS to SFO and $20 SFO to BOS.
- **Stop duration**: fares within the United States typically forbid a stop of more than 4 hours inside an FC.
- **Flight restrictions**: flight numbers, locations and departure times within the FC; "many of the cheapest fares on popular business routes prohibit non-stop travel".
- **Combinability**: H14ESNR requires all fares in the PU to be on AA or TW and prohibits one-way PUs. Fares commonly require same-airline and similar basis codes within the PU.
- **End-on-end**: "Extra-priceable-unit restrictions on fare combinations are called end-on-end fare combinability restrictions". A fare can restrict the basis codes of all other fares on the ticket.
- **Back-to-back**: limits how PUs on the same airline may be embedded, to stop a passenger defeating the Saturday-night rule with two nested round trips (A-B short stay, B-A long stay, A-B short stay, B-A).
- **Companion / accompanied fares**: one passenger's fares constrain another's, which can make the search exponentially harder as passengers are added.
- **Refundability and change penalties** appear only in passing ("permit refunds"; the sample output says "non-refundable", "Changes to this ticket will incur a penalty fee").

Seasonality, blackouts, stopovers and transfers as named categories, and the mileage system, are **not** in the paper.

### 1.5 Complexity results

| Problem | Result | Reduction |
|---|---|---|
| Single fixed fare, fixed route, variable flights | NP-hard | 3SAT. k+1 airports in a chain, two flights per hop (true/false); one fare A to Z whose flight-number restrictions encode the clauses after De Morgan. |
| Fixed flights, fixed PUs, variable fares | NP-hard | Graph k-colouring. One flight per vertex, one fare per (vertex, colour); end-on-end rules forbid the same colour on adjacent vertices. |
| Fixed flights, fixed fares, variable PUs | NP-complete (stated, proof not written up) | "the problem of just validating a printed ticket is worst-case NP-complete", because the PU grouping is not printed on the ticket. |
| Full search, unbounded solution size | EXPSPACE-hard | Simulates a Turing machine. Each flight encodes one tape cell at one time step; one-step consistency is a finite-state constraint built from the flight network and minimum-connection-time tables; multi-step consistency uses round-trip PUs with same-flight-number rules and min stay = max stay = 2 x tape length - 1. n bits of stay encode a tape of length 2^n. |
| Full search | Undecidable | Reduction from Hilbert's tenth problem. Fare counts do unary arithmetic; round-trip PUs enforce equality; the back-to-back restriction supplies the layout constraint needed for unbounded multiplication. |

Other stated points: with a polynomial bound on solution size the problem is NP-hard; whether it is in NP depends on whether IATA checks run in polynomial time. The paper's reading of the results: a system whose long-distance constraints can copy arbitrary amounts of data forward, as PUs can, is liable to be undecidable. Heuristics are risky because airlines can change fares and rules ten times a day.

Demonstrations ran on the unmodified production engine: non-deterministic Turing machines with about 10 states, tape length about 20, about 20 steps, in about 1 second. The incrementer solutions had 98 flights, 98 fares, 36 round-trip PUs and 26 one-way PUs. Binary multiplication topped out at 4 bits (15 x 15) with 130 flights and fares per solution.

### 1.6 Availability as the paper describes it

- Availability is a per-booking-code vector, for example `F1 Y9 B4 H4 W0 Q0 G0`. A fare needs its booking code open on every flight it covers. Counts are capped at 9.
- Booking code is "typically also the first letter of the fare's basis code", chosen by cabin and price.
- Responses move without any bookings, because the demand estimate changes.
- **O&D availability**: many airlines want the whole route of a trip before they give availability for any part of it. The UA131 SEA-DEN example returns `F3 Y9 W2 Q0` for SEA-DEN, `F2 Y9 W1 Q0` for SEA-BOS and `F3 Y9 W5 Q3` for SEA-MIA. Two flights whose availability depends on the passenger taking both are called married.
- The query "must provide not just flight and cabin, but product context", with "future: frequent flyer number". Point of sale is not named, but it is the same mechanism.
- Airline hosts and networks cannot carry 15 million questions a second, so "ITA Software uses distributed, scalable cache". Full-journey availability "would be disastrous for search: too many questions to ask".
- **No locking**: "answer is not guaranteed for any period of time".
- The sample output labels each segment's availability source: `avail checked(live) ... strict-local`, `strict-o&d` with `(married: NW763,NW921)`, and `pseudo-o&d`.
- One remark worth carrying into the simulator: "complicated revenue management techniques only significantly increase profits when planes are flying near capacity."

### 1.7 Search strategy: not in this paper

The complexity notes do not describe ITA's algorithm. The pricing graph comes from two other public sources.

**Patent US 6,275,808**, "Pricing graph representation for sets of pricing solutions for travel planning system" (de Marcken, granted 14 Aug 2001), <https://patents.google.com/patent/US6275808>. **[V]** for the first 100,000 characters of the text:

- Pipeline: scheduler produces itineraries per slice; faring attaches fares; the result is a pricing graph sent to the client, which enumerates and filters locally. Availability filtering "could be included at nearly any point".
- Itineraries are split into **faring atoms** (the flight sequences a single fare could cover), grouped by faring market. Fares and rules are retrieved per atom; each rule returns PASS, FAIL or DEFER, and results are cached per departure date. DEFER postpones rules that need PU or journey context.
- Fares combine into PUs held in factored form: **PU-cores** (fares plus discounts, penalties, surcharges) and **PU-labels** that link sets of faring-atom choices to cores. One label in the patent's example stands for eight PUs.
- Slices are linked through **slice-label-sets** and **open-label-sets** (PU-labels still waiting for a later slice).
- The graph has **OR nodes** (exclusive choice), **AND nodes** (take all children) and **terminal nodes** (itineraries, fares, surcharges, taxes, booking codes). Shared objects become shared nodes.
- Size: a 43-node example represents 856 solutions; "a graph of 10,000 or so nodes can represent more than 1,000,000,000 pricing-solutions".
- Queries run on the graph: inner value (OR = min of children, AND = sum) and outer value give the best total through any node; a validity flag prunes; N-best enumeration uses a priority queue of partial solutions; non-decomposable preferences enter as penalty functions.

Related: US 6,381,578 "Factored representation of a set of priceable units" (<https://patents.google.com/patent/US6381578>) and US 6,377,932 "Rules validation for travel planning system" (<https://patents.google.com/patent/US6377932>). Not read.

**de Marcken's email published by Paul Graham**, <http://www.paulgraham.com/carl.html>. **[V]** via a fetched summary: a BOS-LAX round trip has about 5,000 outbound and 5,000 return routings and more than 10,000 fare combinations per fixed itinerary; the pricing graph represents roughly 10^20 to 10^30 answers and is built in about 10 seconds; each query took 5 to 15 CPU-seconds on a dual 800 MHz Linux box with 2 GB RAM; about 2 GB of static fare and flight data sat in memory-mapped files; the engine was about 200,000 lines of Common Lisp.

Design consequence: enumerate itineraries, evaluate fare-component-scope rules once per (faring atom, fare), defer PU-scope and journey-scope rules, build PUs in factored form, and keep the answer set as an AND/OR graph. Never materialise the cross product.

### 2.4 Booking codes

- Validation order: prime RBD in Record 1, then Chart 1, then Chart 2. The owning carrier's exception outranks a secondary carrier's (Record 6, Convention 2 before Convention 1). **[V]** Travelport FAQ.
- If a secondary carrier has neither concurred in the owner's Chart 1 nor filed its own Chart 2, ATPCO's data application fails the fare. Some GDSs fall back to the prime RBD, which Travelport calls non-compliant. **[V]**
- **Table 999 dual inventory** **[V]**: restriction tag B means "if RBD 1 is available, RBD 2 is permitted"; tag D means RBD 2 is required. Valid only in Record 1 and Chart 1. Example: a T fare on LON-HKG that prices only while N is also available on the flight.
- ATPCO's dynamic-pricing material describes the same device as **Dual RBD Validation**, raising usable price points "from 26 to 182". **[V]** search excerpt of <https://atpco.net/?p=7053>. The excerpt does not show how 182 is derived.

### 2.5 Routings, mileage and construction checks

Mileage system, **[V]** from <https://support.travelport.com/webhelp/FaresAndPricing/Content/Mileage.htm> and <https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/iata-foundation/international-fare-construction-and-ticketing/mileage-system-and-fare-basis-codes>:

- **TPM**: shortest operated mileage between ticketed points; sum over sectors of the fare component.
- **MPM**: 120% of the shortest operated mileage between component origin and destination. Separate MPMs per global direction.
- **EMA**: extra mileage allowance, a deduction for specified via points. Only the single best one applies.
- **EMS**: ratio = (sum of TPM - EMA) / MPM.

| Ratio | Code | Fare multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| up to 1.00 | M | 1.00 |
| above 1.00 to 1.05 | 5M | 1.05 |
| to 1.10 | 10M | 1.10 |
| to 1.15 | 15M | 1.15 |
| to 1.20 | 20M | 1.20 |
| to 1.25 | 25M | 1.25 |
| above 1.25 | fail | break into separate fare components |

Worked examples from those pages: LAX-PHX-HNL-OSA is 370 + 2,914 + 4,106 = 7,390 TPM, less 800 EMA = 6,590 against MPM 6,865, so no surcharge. LHR-SIN: 7,420 less 400 EMA = 7,020 against MPM 6,500, ratio 1.08, 10M, NUC 1,200 becomes NUC 1,320. The surcharge applies to the base fare, not to taxes or Q surcharges.

A fare is either a **routing fare** (specified routing map; no mileage test) or a **mileage fare**. Travelport fails anything beyond 25M and suggests the carrier file a diagrammatic routing if it wants longer paths **[V]**.

Construction order for a through fare **[V]** (Travelport): MPM, TPM, EMA, EMS, HIP, then BHC / CTM / RWM.

| Check | Meaning | Status |
|---|---|---|

Travelport applies it when there is an intermediate **stopover**; Category 17 can waive it. | **[V]** trigger and waiver; **[R]** full definition |
| BHC, one-way backhaul | one-way components where origin-to-intermediate exceeds origin-to-destination. Minimum = origin-to-HIP fare plus (origin-to-HIP fare minus origin-to-destination fare). | **[V]** formula via search excerpt |
| CTM, circle trip minimum | a circle trip may not cost less than the highest round-trip fare from origin to any stopover point | **[R]** |

Other construction facts **[V]**:

- **Fare selection** (<https://support.travelport.com/webhelp/FaresAndPricing/Content/Fare%20Selection.htm>): the fare-owning carrier is the one on the first sector crossing an IATA area, then sub-area, then the first international sector. Within Europe, the lower of that carrier's fare and the longest-TPM carrier's fare. Within US/Canada, the first-sector carrier. YY (IATA) fares apply where the carrier filed none.
- **Direction**: international fares apply from the country of origin, so the inbound half of a round trip is priced with the origin-to-destination fare, against the direction of travel (<https://support.travelport.com/webhelp/GWS/Content/TRANSACTIONHELP/1API_Dev_Notes/TravelIndustryAirlinesandAirfares.pdf>).
- **Global indicators**: AT (Area 1 to Areas 2 or 3 via the Atlantic), PA (Area 1 to Area 3 via the Pacific), AP (Area 2 to Area 3 via both), EH (within the Eastern Hemisphere), WH (within Area 1). Others in use: TS trans-Siberian, FE, RU, PO polar, SA South Atlantic **[R]**.
- **NUC and ROE**: fares are converted to neutral units of construction for the checks, then back to the currency of the country of commencement at the IATA rate of exchange. Round-trip normal fare is twice the one-way in NUC. The fare calculation line shows `NUC ... END ROE ...`. **[V]** concept; ROE publication cadence not checked.

### 2.6 Constructed fares

- An **add-on** (arbitrary, proportional) is an amount that is only ever combined with a published international fare to construct an unpublished one. Add-ons generally cannot be sold alone and are usually between points in one country. **[V]** ATPCO glossary excerpts.
- A constructed fare = specified fare + one add-on, or two add-ons around one specified fare. **[V]** excerpt of patent application US 2012/0084233.
- Each add-on record names the geographic zone and fare-class group of the specified fares it may attach to. **[V]** glossary excerpt.
- US regulation defines an add-on the same way, 14 CFR 221.3. **[V]** excerpt.
- Fare By Rule does not create constructed fares, though a constructed fare can be the base for a discount. **[V]** Travelport FAQ.

### 2.7 YQ/YR, optional services, baggage, brands

**Carrier-imposed fees (YQ/YR)**, **[V]** <https://support.travelport.com/webhelp/FaresAndPricing/Content/YQ-YR.htm>:

- **S1** is filed and owned by the **marketing carrier** and holds the fee sequences. **S2** is the concurrence record filed by the **validating carrier**, naming the carriers it will or will not collect for.

Analysts quoted: "We sometimes have $1 base fares and make it up on YQ/YR"; YQ can be "over half the ticket revenue on a leisure ticket sale". **[V]** <https://info.atpco.net/data-love-story>

### 2.8 Dynamic pricing mechanisms

ATPCO's three tiers, **[V]** search excerpts of <https://atpco.net/?p=7053> and <https://atpco.net/resource/simplified-model-for-dynamic-pricing>:

| Tier | Definition |
|---|---|
| Optimized Pricing | predefined products and price points with dynamic availability. Dual RBD validation belongs here. |
| Adjusted Pricing | the airline's dynamic pricing engine chooses among pre-filed fares or adjusts a filed product. |
| Continuous Pricing | price selected "from a continuous range of possible values"; product creation and price selection merge into one step. |

T2RL's five levels, **[V]** <https://www.t2rl.net/docs/Dynamic%20Pricing%20-%20T2RL%20Definitions.pdf>:

1. Dynamic availability: RBD availability adjusted at request time; pricing still filed.
2. Dynamic availability with price-driven strategies: filed fares, with "hidden" fare rules steering which fare applies.
3. Dynamic pricing with internal fare adjustments: a discount applied when the pricing record is built; "only valid in direct and NDC channels".
4. Dynamic pricing with automated filed-fare updates: offers analysed and pushed back into filed fares or Category 25 discounts for indirect channels.
5. True continuous pricing: "Does not use filed fares or RBD availability."

ATPCO set a 2022 goal of 80% of airline offers dynamically created by 2026 **[V]** (altexsoft). Lufthansa Group began continuous pricing in NDC and direct channels around 2020 **[R]**.

### 3.2 Why Value Pricing and SimpliFares failed, mechanically

The MIT simulations in section 4.6 give the number. Starting from a fully restricted six-class structure with leg EMSRb on both carriers, removing the Saturday-night stay alone costs 16.5% of revenue; removing all restrictions while keeping advance purchase costs 29.6%; removing everything costs 45%. A structure with few fences depends on capacity control to keep high-WTP passengers out of low fares, and independent-demand RM systems cannot do that (spiral-down). Value Pricing also needed every competitor to hold the structure; a weaker carrier gains by undercutting.

## 4. Revenue management science and practice

Verified material in this section comes mainly from Belobaba's MIT 16.75J lecture notes (2006), which include his IATA 2002 and PROS 2006 conference decks: index at <https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-75j-airline-management-spring-2006/pages/lecture-notes/>.

### 4.1 Leg-based control

- **Littlewood (1972)**, "Forecasting and control of passenger bookings", AGIFORS Symposium Proceedings. Two classes: keep selling the low fare while f2 >= f1 x P(D1 > seats remaining). **[R]** for the citation; the rule is the n = 1 case of EMSR.
- **Belobaba**, MIT PhD thesis 1987, "Air Travel Demand and Airline Seat Inventory Management" (EMSRa); EMSRb presented at ORSA/TIMS 1992. **[V]** <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_marginal_seat_revenue>
- **EMSRb** **[V]** (lecture 18, <https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-75j-airline-management-spring-2006/2e0689d14860f52fbd0ea84a07d48048_lect18.pdf>): aggregate classes 1..n into one demand with mean = sum of means, variance = sum of variances, fare = demand-weighted mean fare. Protect pi_n where weighted fare x P(aggregate demand > pi_n) = fare of class n+1. Booking limit for class n+1 = capacity - pi_n.
- Worked example: Y (mean 10, sd 3, $1,000), B (15, 5, $700), M (20, 7, $500), Q (30, 10, $350). Protect 8 for Y; protect 23 for Y+B (weighted fare $820, sd 5.83). With authorised capacity 80: limits Y 80, B 72, M 57.
- Assumptions: independent class demands; low before high; static. EMSRb was within 0.5% of optimal in Belobaba's tests; EMSRa could be off by more than 1.5%. **[V]** Wikipedia.
- Scale: 2,000 legs a day x 10 classes x 300 days is 6 million booking limits. **[V]**
- A "third-generation" RM system: historical bookings by flight and class, forecasts, no-show forecasts, optimiser, overbooking model, analyst review; limits revised at fixed checkpoints (16 in PODS). "Substantial proportion of fare mix revenue gain comes from dynamic revision of booking limits." **[V]**
- Why revenue, not yield or load: on a 200-seat, 2,100-mile leg, yield emphasis gives 55% load and $28,940; load emphasis gives 80% and $30,160; revenue emphasis gives 68% and $31,250 at 10.94 cents per RPM. **[V]**

### 4.2 American Airlines

Smith, Leimkuhler and Darrow, "Yield Management at American Airlines", *Interfaces* 22(1), 1992, pp. 8-31. **[V]** abstract summary via <https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/orinte/v22y1992i1p8-31.html>: research on managing reservations inventory began in the early 1960s; the problem was split into **overbooking, discount allocation and traffic management**; benefit estimated at **$1.4 billion over three years**, with more than **$500 million a year** expected to continue. The work won the 1991 Edelman Prize. DINAMO (Dynamic Inventory and Maintenance Optimizer) as the implementing system, and virtual nesting ("indexing") as the traffic-management mechanism, are **[R]**; the paper is paywalled.

Crandall on yield management: "the single most important technical development in transportation management since we entered deregulation". **[V]** Wikipedia.

### 4.3 Network (O&D) control

**[V]** lecture 20 (<https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-75j-airline-management-spring-2006/021cc8536da3b1ab8d8dba2c90e3a486_lect20.pdf>) and the IATA 2002 deck (<https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-75j-airline-management-spring-2006/9998cb453ec6ac724bc938320809a07b_lect24.pdf>).

O&D control is the ability to answer different O-D requests with different seat availability on the same itinerary.

| System | Data and forecasts | Optimiser | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| FCYM (base) | leg / class | leg EMSR | leg / class limits |
| Revenue value buckets | leg / bucket | leg EMSR | leg / bucket limits ("greedy"; over-favours long haul) |
| Heuristic bid price (HBP) | leg / bucket | leg EMSR | bid price for connections only |
| DAVN | O&D itinerary fare | network LP + leg EMSR | leg / virtual bucket limits |
| Network bid price (ProBP) | O&D itinerary fare | probabilistic network convergence | additive O&D bid prices |

- **Virtual nesting**: each O&D fare is mapped to a virtual bucket on each leg by value. The example uses 10 buckets on NCE-FRA from "$1,200+" down to "$0-149".
- **Displacement adjustment**: network value on a leg = total fare minus displacement cost on the other legs.
- **Bid price**: accept if fare > sum of leg bid prices. Example with A-B $35, B-C $240, C-D $160: on A-D (bid $435) only Y $582 is open; on A-C (bid $275) Y, M and B are open.
- **HBP**: connecting bid = max(EMSR1, EMSR2) + d x min(EMSR1, EMSR2), with 0 < d < 1.
- "CRS seamless availability links are essential" for anything beyond value buckets inside existing classes.

### 4.4 Forecasting and unconstraining

- Baseline in PODS: **pickup** (moving average of bookings still to come) with **booking-curve detruncation** of closed observations. **[V]**
- Better: **projection detruncation** (Hopperstad's iterative method) and **regression** on bookings in hand. **[V]**
- The reason they pay: forecasts become larger, which protects more seats, not because they are more accurate. **[V]**
- Demand variability: coefficient of variation ("k-factor") of total flight demand is "typically between 0.20 and 0.40", higher in leisure markets; 0.35 used as typical. Boeing spill table at k = 0.35: an observed 85.0% load factor implies a demand factor of 0.972 and a spill factor of 0.122. **[V]** <https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-75j-airline-management-spring-2006/f990b2cd2141f75cd9b348051af762e7_lect4b.pdf>
- Exponential smoothing and EM unconstraining (Weatherford and Polt, 2002) **[R]**.

### 4.6 Less restricted fares: spiral-down, hybrid forecasting, fare adjustment

|---|---|---|---|
| Fully restricted | base | 81.6% | 8.2% |
| Remove advance purchase only | -0.8% | 87.8% | 9.9% |
| Remove Saturday-night stay only | -16.5% | 79.8% | 11.8% |

The percentages are read from garbled chart labels; the order of magnitude is certain, the decimal places less so. The last row is the result that matters: with no fences, an unmodified RM system adds nothing.

**Spiral-down**: Cooper, Homem-de-Mello and Kleywegt, "Models of the Spiral-Down Effect in Revenue Management", *Operations Research* 54(5), 2006. When protection levels are set from forecasts built on observed sales, and customers buy the lowest open fare, low high-fare sales lead to lower protection, which leads to still lower high-fare sales. **[R]** for the journal paper. **[V]** for the model and its example 5, from the authors' TRISTAN V abstract of 2004, <https://tristanconference.org/system/tristan-v/website/pdf/abstract_00098.pdf>: capacity 100, fares 1 and 0.7, arrivals at rate 1.5 over a horizon of 100, the first third buying class 2 only, the second third either class, the last third class 1 only; the manager's model gives a protection level of 70 (the 0.3 quantile of a Poisson of 75), the optimum is 95, and with observed class-1 sales read as demand the protection levels go to zero. jetway's test reproduces 70 and 95, and the spiral. Belobaba's description **[V]**: "Unless demand forecasts are adjusted to reflect potential sell-up, high-fare demand will be consistently under-forecast. Optimizer then under-protects, allowing more 'spiral down'."

**Hybrid forecasting** **[V]** (lecture 26): classify each historical booking as *product-oriented* (yieldable: the next lower class was open, so the passenger chose this product) or *price-oriented* (priceable: the next lower class was closed). Forecast the first with standard time series by class. Forecast the second as total demand potential at the lowest fare, converted to demand by fare level using estimated sell-up.

Results in PODS Network D (2 hub airlines, 40 spokes, 252 legs, 482 O&D markets, baseline load factor 86.45%):

| Case | Airline 1 revenue | Airline 2 revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Airline 1 adopts hybrid forecasting, Airline 2 standard | +3.4% (load factor -0.75 pt) | -0.2% |
| Both adopt | +2.97% | +3.03% |

Gain over standard-forecast EMSRb, simplified fares:

| Method | Standard forecasting | With hybrid forecasting |
|---|---|---|
| EMSRb | base | +2.6% |
| DAVN | +0.74% | +4.0% |
| ProBP | +0.43% | +3.3% |
| HBP | +1.93% | +3.7% |

In the larger Network R (4 airlines, 4 hubs), with hybrid forecasting: EMSRb +1.3%, DAVN +2.4%, ProBP +3.0%, HBP +2.4%, against +1.51%, +0.65% and +0.44% for DAVN, ProBP and HBP on standard forecasts. Network optimisers suffer most from independent-demand forecasts, because they over-protect for low-class connecting traffic that will not arrive.

**Q-forecasting** (Belobaba and Hopperstad, 2004) is the fully unrestricted case: forecast demand at the lowest fare Q and partition it by sell-up probability **[R]**.

**Fare adjustment** (Fiig, Isler, Hopperstad and Belobaba, "Optimization of mixed fare structures: Theory and applications", *Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management* 9, 2010, pp. 152-170) **[V]** <https://amadeus.com/documents/en/research-papers/rpm200918a.pdf>. Read on 20 November 2026. The paper's general form: a strategy is a set of open classes; Q(Z) is its total demand and TR(Z) its total revenue; the strategies on the upper convex hull of (Q, TR) from the origin are efficient; each efficient strategy k has partitioned demand Q_k - Q_(k-1) and adjusted fare (TR_k - TR_(k-1)) / (Q_k - Q_(k-1)); an independent-demand optimiser given those reproduces the choice-model optimum. EMSRb-MR is three steps: find the efficient strategies, transform, apply EMSRb. Table 1 (six fares 1200 to 200, demands 31.2, 10.9, 14.8, 19.9, 26.9, 36.3): undifferentiated marginal revenues 1200, 428, 228, 28, -172, -372. Table 2 (capacity 100, standard deviations 11.2, 6.6, 7.7, 8.9, 10.4, 12.0): EMSRb limits 100, 80, 65, 46, 20, 0; EMSRb-MR limits 100, 65, 48, 16, 0, 0. PODS network D, monopoly: hybrid forecasting +10 to +16% revenue over standard; fare adjustment a further +16%, with load factor falling from 85% to 72-74%. jetway's tests reproduce both tables. Mechanism for the fully undifferentiated case:

- For price-oriented demand, replace each fare by its marginal revenue: f'_k = (f_k x Q_k - f_(k-1) x Q_(k-1)) / (Q_k - Q_(k-1)), where Q_k is demand when k is the lowest open class. Feed f' and the partitioned demands Q_k - Q_(k-1) to an unchanged independent-demand optimiser.
- With exponential sell-up P(pay >= f) = exp(-beta x (f - f_low)), the adjustment is a constant: f' = f - 1/beta.
- Classes whose adjusted fare is negative, or below the bid price, stay closed even on an empty flight. That is what stops spiral-down.
- For mixed structures, product-oriented demand keeps its fare and price-oriented demand gets the adjusted fare; in DAVN the displacement cost is subtracted as well.

**FRAT5** is the fare ratio at which half of passengers will sell up from the lowest fare, so beta = ln 2 / ((FRAT5 - 1) x f_low) and 1/beta = (FRAT5 - 1) x f_low / ln 2. With f_low = $100 and FRAT5 = 2.0, the adjustment is $144. Belobaba's chart of "Willingness to Pay Relative to Lowest Fare" over the 16 booking periods **[V]** (values read off the chart, so approximate): the LOW curve rises from about 1.2 to about 2; MEDIUM from about 1.3 to about 3; HIGH from about 1.5 to about 5. All are S-shaped and steepest in the last five periods. Estimation needs observations of bookings when lower classes were closed: "we can't estimate what we've never seen".

### 4.7 The PODS simulator

**[V]** lectures 6, 7 and 23 (<https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-75j-airline-management-spring-2006/04374703df2aaeeb5f89950400d75757_lect6a.pdf>).

- Built at Boeing in the early 1990s from the Decision Window Model; developed with MIT since 1994; funded by a consortium of seven airlines (2002).
- Loop: passenger decision model, then path/class bookings and cancellations, then each airline's historical database, forecaster and optimiser, then path/class availability back to the passenger model. Each airline's RM system sees only its own past bookings, and its own earlier limits shape that history.
- Many samples of the same departure day; stationary demand; early samples discarded as burn-in.
- **16 time frames** before departure, with boundaries at 63, 56, 49, 42, 35, 31, 28, 24, 21, 17, 14, 10, 7, 5, 3 and 1 days out. RM intervenes at the start of each; bookings arrive randomly within it; the database updates at the end.
- Demand inputs: mean daily demand per O&D market by passenger type; a booking curve per type; correlation between types and across periods.
- Passenger generation: draw a decision window (earliest departure, latest arrival) from the market's time-of-day profile; draw maximum willingness to pay; draw restriction disutilities from normal distributions around type means.
- **WTP**: P(pay at least f) = min[1, exp(-ln 2 x (f - basefare) / ((emult - 1) x basefare))]. Basefare = Q fare for leisure, 2.5 x Q for business. emult = **1.2 leisure, 3 business**. Half of each type will pay emult x basefare. Stated consequence: about 20% of leisure passengers will pay a higher fare; most business passengers will pay Y.
- **Choice**: discard path/fare options priced above WTP or unavailable; choose the minimum of fare + restriction disutilities + path quality + replanning + unfavourite airline.
- Mean parameters at Y $800 / M $400 / B $300 / Q $200:

| | Business | Leisure |
|---|---|---|
| Mean maximum pay | $1,200 | $300 |
| Saturday-night stay | $450 | $350 |
| Non-refundable | $150 | $50 |
| Change fee | $150 | $50 |
| Generalised cost of Y / M / B / Q | 800 / 850 / 900 / 950 | 800 / 750 / 700 / 650 |

  The coefficients were tuned so that business passengers on average rank Y > M > B > Q and leisure passengers Q > B > M > Y.
- Path quality index: 1 for nonstop, 3 for a connection, times a unit cost that scales with the market's base fare. Replanning cost applies outside the decision window. Unfavourite-airline cost exists but is off in ePODS.
- ePODS network: 5 airlines, each with one hub and 20 spokes (10 each side), 40 spoke cities in all, two connecting banks each way per day, 1 to 5 competitors per spoke city, 20 aircraft per airline of 120 / 150 / 180 / 220 seats. Block time = 0.67 h + 0.001967 x miles. Minimum spoke turn 40 minutes. No overbooking or no-shows in ePODS.
- Emmanuel Carrier's 2003 MIT thesis argues the PODS choice process is approximated by a mixed logit, not a standard logit, and replaces constant replanning cost with one that grows with schedule inconvenience. **[V]** abstract, <https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/16916>

## 5. Availability by channel

Verified statements are marked; the rest is **[R]** practitioner knowledge, because the vendor documentation that describes it sits behind logins.

### 5.1 How a channel learns availability

| Level | Mechanism | What the seller sees | Fidelity |
|---|---|---|---|
| AVS, availability status | Type B teletype status messages per flight, date and class (open, closed, request, waitlist; leg- or segment-qualified) | open or closed, shown as a default count | no journey, no point of sale; airline closes at a threshold |
| NAVS, numeric AVS | pushed seat counts per class | 0-9 per class | per segment only |
| Direct access / carrier-specific display | agent queries one carrier's host | that host's view | live, single carrier |
| Seamless / interactive polling | GDS or engine asks the host in real time over EDIFACT (availability request and response; interactive sell) | live counts for the requested journey | journey and point of sale can be sent |
| Cache | engine stores earlier answers | possibly stale | depends on refresh and key |
| Availability calculator / proxy | airline replicates bid prices and rules to a high-volume service off the host | computed answer | as good as the replication |
| Direct / NDC offer | airline prices the offer itself | a price, maybe no class | full context |

- A GDS availability line shows classes with a number or a letter (A available, C closed), capped at 9, and two characters describing "how the availability was retrieved". **[V]** Travelport overview PDF.
- Type A (EDIFACT, interactive) and Type B (teletype) both run over SITA's network. **[V]** <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_reservations_system>
- O&D control needs seamless links: "Different responses to different ODF requests". **[V]** Belobaba.
- No lock between availability and sell; sells can be rejected. **[V]** de Marcken.
- Cost: 15 million questions a second if every search polled. **[V]** de Marcken. Hence caches, look-to-book limits in API contracts, and calculators.
- ITA's engine recorded per segment whether availability was checked live, and whether it was `strict-local`, `strict-o&d` or `pseudo-o&d`. **[V]**

### 5.2 Journey, point of sale and married segments

- With polling, the request carries the whole connection and the seller's identity (country, city, agency number, channel). The host values the request as fare for that O&D and point of sale, minus displacement, against bid prices.
- The same seat differs by country or channel for four reasons: (1) the applicable fare differs by point of sale and currency, so the O&D value differs; (2) the airline applies point-of-sale adjustments to bid prices or class availability to block cross-border arbitrage; (3) Category 15 and Category 35 restrict who may sell which fare; (4) the channel is on AVS or a stale cache.
- **Married segments**: when a connection is sold as a unit the segments are flagged married and may not be cancelled or changed separately. **[V]** de Marcken for the definition.
- Abuse: selling two local segments in a class closed for the through journey, or the reverse, then pricing at the through fare. **[V]** Belobaba: two local legs are booked when the connecting itinerary is not available and are then priced at the through O-D fare in the same class, growing as "web site and GDS pricing search engines look for lowest fare itineraries"; a 25% abuse rate removes up to a third of DAVN's gain.
- Countermeasures: journey control at sell time (re-evaluate the whole PNR), refusal to price broken marriages, robots that cancel, and ADMs.

|---|---|

## 6. Tactics that rules exist to prevent

| Tactic | How it works | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden-city (point beyond) | buy A-B-C because it is cheaper than A-B; leave at B. One-way, carry-on only. | conditions of carriage; cancellation of onward segments; ADM to the agent; loyalty-account action |
| Throwaway | buy a round trip cheaper than the one-way; skip the return | sequence-of-coupon rule; repricing |
| Back-to-back (nested) | two round trips A-B-A and B-A-B interleaved so each has a Saturday night | the back-to-back restriction, which limits how PUs on one airline may be embedded **[V]** de Marcken |

| Fuel dumping | add an unrelated segment or fare component so the carrier-imposed-fee records no longer match the long-haul sectors | sector-level S1 coding; ADM |
| Churning | cancel and rebook repeatedly to extend the ticketing deadline | robots counting cancel-rebook cycles; ADM per segment |
| Speculative and duplicate bookings | hold space without intent to ticket | ticketing time limits from Category 5 and from host robots; duplicate checks; auto-cancel |
| Fictitious names | placeholder names to hold seats | name-pattern robots; ADM |
| Inactive or passive segment abuse | leaving dead segments, or passive segments used to issue tickets | per-segment ADM |
| Mistake fares | filing errors (dropped digit, missing surcharge, currency) | DOT has allowed cancellation with reimbursement of expenses since 2015 **[R]** |

### (b) Passenger choice model

Use the PODS form; it is the only model with published parameters that has been shown to make competing RM systems behave plausibly.

**Segments**: business and leisure at minimum. Business share by market 20% to 50% ESTIMATE. A third "visiting friends and relatives" segment with leisure WTP and business-like timing is an optional ESTIMATE.

**Willingness to pay** (verified form and constants): P(pay >= f) = min[1, exp(-ln 2 x (f - base) / ((emult - 1) x base))]; leisure base = lowest fare, emult 1.2; business base = 2.5 x lowest fare, emult 3.

**Restriction disutilities**, as multiples of the lowest fare, from the verified $200 example: Saturday-night stay 2.25 business / 1.75 leisure; non-refundable 0.75 / 0.25; change fee 0.75 / 0.25. Draw each per passenger from a normal distribution around the mean; standard deviation of 30% of the mean is an ESTIMATE.

**Path costs**: connection penalty = 2 x unit path-quality cost (index 3 vs 1), with unit cost ESTIMATE 0.1 x base fare for leisure and 0.3 x for business; replanning cost outside the decision window; a small unfavourite-airline cost to model loyalty.

**Market-level elasticity** for scaling total demand when the whole market's price level moves: -1.4 at route level times the regional multiplier in section 4.8; -0.7 business and -1.5 leisure on short haul; -0.27 business and -1.04 leisure on long-haul international.

**Variability**: coefficient of variation of total flight demand 0.20 to 0.40, 0.35 typical (verified); higher for leisure. Correlate demand positively across time frames and across segments.

**Sell-up for the airlines' estimators**: FRAT5 rising in an S-curve from 1.2-1.5 at 63 days to 2.5-3.0 at departure in mixed markets; 1.5-2.0 at departure in leisure markets; 4-5 in business markets (chart reading, section 4.6).

**No-shows and cancellations**: no-show 10-15% of final bookings on flexible fares (verified range) and ESTIMATE 2-5% on non-refundable fares; cancellations before departure ESTIMATE 10-30% of bookings, higher on flexible fares and earlier bookings.

**Discipline that makes it realistic**: each airline sees only its own censored history; closed classes hide demand; run a burn-in; passengers refused by one airline try their next-best option, so one airline's closures are another's bookings.

### (c) Channel and point-of-sale availability

Make availability a function, not a table: `seats(journey segments, RBD, point of sale {country, city, agency, channel}, party size, time)` returning 0-9.

1. **Airline core**: bid price per leg and day. A request is open if (fare applicable at that point of sale, net of channel cost) minus displacement on other legs >= bid price of this leg. This gives O&D and point-of-sale differences from one mechanism.
2. **Explicit point-of-sale rules** on top: per-country or per-channel multipliers or class caps, plus Category 15 sale restrictions on the fares themselves.
3. **Publication levels per partner or channel**:

| Level | Simulated behaviour |
|---|---|
| AVS | open/closed per segment and class, computed on the **local** value; closed below a threshold (ESTIMATE: fewer than 4 seats); message delay of seconds to minutes |
| NAVS | 0-9 per segment, local value |
| Polled | journey and point of sale honoured; subject to rate limits |
| Cached | any of the above with a time-to-live and a key that may omit point of sale; this is what metasearch sees |
| Direct | offer priced by the airline; may be a continuous price |

4. **Sell-time recheck with rejection**. No lock between look and book. Reject or reprice when the cached answer was wrong. Track look-to-book per channel.
5. **Married-segment flag** set when a connection is sold on journey value; a cancel or change of one segment triggers re-evaluation of the rest.
6. **Partner bucket mapping**: many-to-one tables with unmapped low buckets; free sale up to N seats under AVS, request otherwise; optional bid-price exchange between allied carriers.

### (d) RM refinements after EMSRb and leg bid prices, in order of value

| Order | Refinement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unconstraining of closed observations plus a better forecaster (pickup, then regression or projection) | "Revenue gains almost double"; cheap to build |
| 2 | Cost-based overbooking with no-show and cancellation forecasts | "as much revenue gain as fare class seat allocation"; leg control plus overbooking together are worth 4-6% |
| 3 | Hybrid forecasting with fare adjustment, as soon as any market has a simplified structure | +2.6% to +4.0%; without it EMSRb's gain falls to 0.1% on unrestricted fares and network methods lose most of their edge |
| 4 | O&D control: DAVN with LP displacement costs, or additive bid prices from a probabilistic method | +1-2% over leg control, growing with load factor. DAVN is robust to the optimiser; pure LP bid prices are not. |
| 5 | Frequent re-optimisation (16 or more checkpoints, plus event triggers) | bid-price control "depends on frequent re-optimization" |
| 6 | Journey control and married-segment enforcement | abuse at 25% takes back a third of the O&D gain |
| 7 | Point-of-sale and channel valuation | needed for section (c); no published gain figure found |
| 8 | Competitor-aware sell-up estimates (conditioning on rivals' open classes) | flagged by Belobaba as the open research item in 2006 |
| 9 | Alliance bid-price or displacement sharing | positive joint gain in PODS |
| 10 | Continuous pricing in direct channels | removes the 26-price-point constraint; evidence not sourced this session |

**Built on 20 November 2026** (jetway v0.1.105, wholesky `internal/rm`): rows 1 and 3, as one forecaster. Each carrier keeps a flight's booking record by time frame and by the lowest class on sale. Bookings divided by the time on sale is the unconstraining; this is the maximum-likelihood rate of a Poisson stream observed part of the time, and it was chosen over EM on closure flags because a flag cannot fill a cell that closed on every departure. The marginal revenue transformation is applied frame by frame, and EMSRb pools every increment of demand still to come that is worth more than the class in question. Measured on one US network carrier's 942 flights over 38 days at 1.4 times an ordinary day's demand: sales read as demand lose 4.3% of revenue over 30 days and are still falling; unconstrained class by class holds (+1.4%) but the dearer class of each pair with the same rules stops selling; fare adjusted gains 3.2% and earns 18% more than fixed limits. At an ordinary day's demand fare adjustment earns 4% more than sales read as demand, which is de Marcken's caution below. Not built: sell-up estimated for strategies never tried, forecasts by origin and destination, overbooking.

**Built on 22 November 2026** (jetway v0.1.106, wholesky `internal/rm`): row 2, for no-shows and not for cancellations. Sources read: Belobaba, "Flight Overbooking: Models and Practice", MIT OpenCourseWare 16.75J lecture 19, 2006 (<https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-75j-airline-management-spring-2006/resources/lect19/>), for the terms, the deterministic, probabilistic and cost-based models and their worked figures (AU 125 and 113 for CAP 100; the cost-based charts for CAP 120); GAO-20-191, "Airline Consumer Protections: Information on Airlines' Denied Boarding Practices", December 2019 (<https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-191>), for no-show rates of about 10% of final pre-departure bookings (down from 12 to 15%), 43 voluntary and about 1 involuntary denied boardings per 100,000 boardings in 2018 (about 100 in all in 2012), premium cabins generally not overbooked, 2 of 7 airlines not overbooking, the smaller-aircraft exemption, and an average involuntary compensation of $937 in 2018; 14 CFR 250.5 as amended by 89 FR 84818 (limits $1,075 and $2,150), read on law.cornell.edu because ecfr.gov refused the fetcher; DOT Air Travel Consumer Report, December 2024 release: an involuntary rate of 0.25 per 10,000 in the fourth quarter of 2024. Each carrier keeps who came for each flight's cabin and sets the main cabin's limit by the cost-based model, with a denied boarding at 2 average fares. Travellers stay away from 17% of bookings on fares that can be refunded and 8.5% on the others; that split is an estimate, and the average of 9.5% is the GAO's figure. Measured over the US world of 12 carriers: 0.825 of seats flown full, 4.4 denied boardings per 10,000 carried against the GAO's 4.4 for 2018, with no constant fitted to it. On one network carrier's busy day overbooking carries 6.5% more passengers and earns 1.7% more in fares, which is under the "as much as seat allocation" of the lecture: the marginal seat on these flights sells at a deep discount. At the airport 1 seated passenger in 25 takes the carrier's offer, an estimate; over 1,227 oversold flights of the filled US world (short of 3.7 seats on average) that share gives 11 volunteers to each passenger turned away, between the GAO's 9 to 1 for 2012 and 33 to 1 for 2018. A flown day of a 6-carrier European world at 360 times real time: 1,335 flights carried 144,663 passengers in 183,000 seats (0.79), with 35 denied boardings, 2.4 per 10,000, 33 of them volunteers, and no cabin sold past its limit. A door count is used only when the airport had the whole name list and its counter opened: a world that is still writing the day's bookings when an early flight's list goes closes that flight on part of its passengers, and neither the scorecard nor the record of who came takes such a count. Not built: cancellations inside the booking window, a no-show forecast by passenger, the refund of a no-show's refundable fare, and the denied passenger's later flight. The demand volume and the fare ratio were fitted again (pool 100 travellers for each 67 passengers carried, mix ratio 1.335), because the report's load factor is of passengers carried.

One caution from de Marcken applies to all of it: sophisticated RM "only significantly increase[s] profits when planes are flying near capacity". The simulator needs demand factors near or above 1.0 on peak flights for any of these differences to show.
